AN IDEAS FEATURE · APPROX. 640 WORDS

The Road to Nowhere

AImagine a city council celebrating the opening of a new road. The ribbon is cut, photographers gather, and the mayor promises that commuters will finally spend less time trapped in traffic. For a few weeks, the promise appears to hold. Then the queues return. Worse still, some residents now spend longer travelling than they did before the road was built. Has the city made a mistake, or have its drivers behaved in a way no planner could have anticipated?

BPart of the answer lies in what transport economists call induced demand. A road is not simply a container waiting to be filled with a fixed number of cars. When travel becomes easier, people change their behaviour. Some commuters switch from trains to cars; others move farther from their workplaces or begin making journeys they previously avoided. A wider road may therefore offer short-lived relief without providing a lasting solution. In effect, the apparent convenience helps create the very traffic that eventually undermines it.

CThere is a second, stranger possibility. In 1968 the German mathematician Dietrich Braess described a network in which adding a new connection could make everybody's journey longer. The puzzle does not require selfishness in a moral sense, let alone irrationality. Each driver merely selects the route that seems quickest. But when thousands make the same individually sensible choice, their decisions interact: the new shortcut becomes attractive, traffic concentrates on the roads leading to it, and the entire system can perform worse. The phenomenon illustrates a counterintuitive truth: what benefits an individual in isolation need not benefit a group.

“More capacity is not automatically the same as better access.”

DConsider a deliberately simplified model with 4,000 drivers and two routes, each containing one road that always takes 45 minutes and one whose journey time depends on how many people use it. Initially, drivers divide equally between the two routes, and a trip takes 65 minutes. Now introduce a free shortcut between the middle junctions. Every driver has an incentive to use both congestion-sensitive roads, and the new equilibrium is 80 minutes per trip. Nothing is physically wrong with the extra link. The problem is that the choices it encourages are collectively costly. Of course, this is a mathematical illustration, not a forecast for an actual city.

EIt would be a mistake, however, to turn this paradox into a slogan that all new roads are harmful. An improved bridge may provide a vital connection to a hospital, and a bypass may remove heavy vehicles from residential streets. Geography, travel patterns, public-transport alternatives and the design of the wider network all matter. Braess’s paradox describes what can occur, not what must occur. Good policy requires evidence rather than a dramatic example used as a universal rule.

FYet even a project that makes travel faster raises a difficult question: faster for whom? A motorway might help drivers while making a neighbourhood less pleasant for pedestrians. A congestion charge might discourage car trips but disproportionately burden people who have no realistic alternative. Equally, frequent buses and protected cycle routes may improve access for households that cannot afford a vehicle. Judging a policy solely by average vehicle speed risks overlooking whose time, money and opportunities are being counted.

GPerhaps, then, the most revealing question is not how many roads a city can build, but what its residents need to reach. Schools, jobs, friends and essential services are destinations, not speeds on a dashboard. A city designed around access could choose better public transport, safer streets or changes to land use; in some circumstances, it could still choose a new road. The key is to compare trade-offs rather than mistake an attractive shortcut for a complete answer. The shortest route to progress is not always a road.

KEY DISTINCTION
Mobility ≠ Access

Mobility is movement. Access is the ability to reach the things that matter.

READER'S RADAR
can ≠ must

Watch for qualified claims. Olympiad questions often exploit the gap between “may happen” and “always happens”.

CRITICAL READING
Who benefits?

A claim can be statistically true but conceal the uneven distribution of its consequences.
